The objective is for the viewer (judge, jury, investigator, or opposing counsel) to naturally arrive at the legal conclusion from the facts presented on the screen.  Every screen MUST follow this structure  1. Headline 2. Evidence 3. Context 4. Timeline 5. Observable Facts 6. Legal Connection 7. Why It Matters  If any section is missing, reject generation.  ⸻  1. Headline  Headlines must summarize observable events.  Allowed:  * Supervisor Confirmed Plaintiff Before Contradicting Himself * Statement Made After Prior Investigation * Timeline Shows Prior Knowledge  Not Allowed:  * Corrupt Supervisor * He Lied * Obvious Cover-Up * Criminal Conduct  The headline should still be true even if every adjective is removed.  ⸻  2. Evidence  Every screen must contain at least one primary source.  Acceptable evidence:  * Transcript quote * Audio * Video * Email * Report * Body camera * Policy * Timeline * GPS * CAD * Dispatch log * Photograph * Screenshot  No evidence = reject screen.  ⸻  3. Context  Explain why the evidence matters.  Example:  Before making this statement, Supervisor had already interviewed both parties.  Context should never speculate.  ⸻  4. Timeline  Every screen should establish chronology.  Event A ↓ Event B ↓ Statement ↓ Decision ↓ Result  Chronology creates persuasion.  ⸻  5. Observable Facts  Only describe what can actually be observed.  Allowed:  * Supervisor stated… * Driver reported… * Plaintiff explained… * Policy required… * Video shows…  Not allowed:  * Supervisor intended… * Supervisor wanted… * Supervisor believed… * Supervisor fabricated…  unless those conclusions are supported by explicit evidence shown on the screen.  ⸻  6. Legal Connection  Only after facts have been presented.  Examples:  “This evidence supports Count 1 because…”  “This evidence relates to the First Amendment retaliation claim because…”  “This evidence supports deliberate indifference because…”  Never state liability as established fact.  Always explain which legal element the evidence tends to support.  ⸻  7. Why It Matters  Maximum 2–3 sentences.  Explain why the evidence is legally significant.  Do not repeat facts.  Explain significance.  ⸻  Automatic Validation Rules  Reject the screen if:  * No evidence exists. * No timeline exists. * No context exists. * Legal conclusion appears before evidence. * Screen contains unsupported accusations. * Screen contains speculation. * Screen contains emotional language unsupported by evidence. * Screen lacks a “Why It Matters” section.  ⸻  Objectivity Score  Each generated screen receives an Objectivity Score (0–100).  Scoring:  +20 Primary evidence  +15 Timeline  +15 Context  +15 Observable facts  +15 Legal connection  +10 Why It Matters  +10 Proper citations  Reject any screen scoring below 90.  ⸻  Persuasion Score  Each screen also receives a Persuasion Score based on clarity rather than emotion.  Higher scores require:  * Minimal text * Clear hierarchy * Strong chronology * Large evidence * Short factual bullets * Easy visual scanning  Lower scores for:  * Dense paragraphs * Repetition * Unsupported conclusions * Excessive adjectives * Emotional wording  ⸻  The “Delete the Headline” Test  The system should simulate removing the headline.  If the remaining screen still leads a reasonable reader to the same conclusion based solely on the evidence, the screen passes.  If not, reject the screen.  ⸻  Golden Rule  The screen should never persuade because it tells the viewer what to think.  The screen should persuade because the evidence leaves the viewer with only one reasonable conclusion.  The evidence—not the graphics, not the adjectives, and not the narration—should carry the argument.  ⸻  I would also add one final rule for your app, because it’s the one that separates professional trial graphics from ordinary presentations:  Every visual element must earn its place by improving comprehension. If a box, icon, color, animation, or headline doesn’t make the evidence easier to understand or the sequence easier to follow, it should be removed. The goal isn’t to make the screen look persuasive—it’s to make the facts unmistakably clear. That’s the standard that tends to resonate best with judges and juries.
